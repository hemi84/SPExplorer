3D Self-Potential Data Inversion: Theory, Methodology, and Applications in Mineral Exploration, Geothermal Systems, and Subsurface Characterization

SPExplorer Geoscience Technical Team · 2024

Self-Potential SP Inversion Source Current Density Sulphide Exploration Geothermal Systems Electrokinetics Geophysical Inversion Mineral Exploration Tongariro Volcanic Centre
Abstract

Self-Potential (SP) is one of the oldest geophysical methods, yet recent advances in computational power and inversion algorithms have transformed SP from a qualitative anomaly-mapping technique into a quantitative three-dimensional imaging tool. SP anomalies arise from natural electric fields generated by electrochemical, electrokinetic, thermoelectric, and redox processes occurring within the subsurface. Modern 3D inversion enables estimation of the spatial distribution of source current density responsible for observed surface potentials, providing valuable information regarding fluid flow, mineralization, and subsurface electrochemical activity.

This paper presents the theoretical framework, numerical implementation, and practical applications of 3D SP inversion. The inversion methodology employs Green's function forward modelling, topography-aware active cell selection, depth weighting, smoothness-constrained regularization, and large-scale iterative solvers. A field validation case study from the Tongariro Volcanic Centre, New Zealand, demonstrates recovery of volumetric source current density on a 335,104-cell model with an achieved RMS misfit of 31.4 mV, resolving hydrothermal upflow conduits and electrokinetic flow pathways to depths exceeding 800 m below surface.

1. Introduction

The Self-Potential (SP) method measures naturally occurring electrical potential differences at the Earth's surface. Unlike induced polarization (IP), resistivity, or electromagnetic methods, SP requires no artificial current injection, making it one of the lowest-cost geophysical techniques available. Historically, SP surveys were primarily used to locate massive sulphide deposits through detection of oxidation-reduction (redox) processes associated with conductive ore bodies.

Advances in hydrogeophysics and electrokinetic theory have demonstrated that SP anomalies arise from a broader range of subsurface processes:

  • Groundwater flow (streaming potential / electrokinetic effect)
  • Geothermal and hydrothermal circulation
  • Hydrothermal alteration mineralogy
  • Contaminant plume migration (diffusion potential)
  • Corrosion processes in infrastructure
  • Fracture-controlled fluid movement

The principal limitation of traditional SP interpretation has been its qualitative nature — coloured anomaly maps that admit multiple geological interpretations. Modern 3D inversion methods overcome this limitation by reconstructing the subsurface distribution of source current density responsible for observed surface potentials, converting SP data into a quantitative, geologically interpretable three-dimensional model.

2. Physical Basis of Self-Potential Signals
2.1 Governing Equations

Under quasi-static conditions, electrical current conservation requires the total current density to be divergence-free:

∇ · J = 0

Current density is decomposed into a passive ohmic component and an active source component:

J = −σ∇ϕ + Jₛ

where σ is electrical conductivity (S·m⁻¹), ϕ is electric potential (V), and Jₛ is the source current density (A·m⁻²). Substituting into the conservation equation yields the SP forward problem:

∇ · (σ∇ϕ) = ∇ · Jₛ = Qₛ

The scalar quantity Qₛ = ∇ · Jₛ (A·m⁻³) is the source current density divergence recovered by inversion — positive values represent current sources and negative values represent current sinks.

2.2 The Sulphide Geobattery Model

A critical and often misunderstood point: the measured surface SP anomaly above a buried sulphide body is typically negative (−50 to −1,000 mV), while the oxidation zone (upper ore body) behaves electrochemically as an anode — releasing electrons that travel through the conductive sulphide toward a deeper reduction zone. The recovered source current density Qₛ in the oxidation zone is correspondingly negative (current sink), consistent with anodic electron release.

3. 3D Inversion Methodology
3.1 Forward Modelling

The forward problem is solved using the Green's function approach. For a half-space with uniform or layered conductivity, the surface potential due to a volumetric source distribution Qₛ(r') is:

ϕ(r) = ∫∫∫_V G(r, r') · Qₛ(r') dV'

where G(r, r') is the Green's function for the geometry. In practice, the volume integral is discretised over a structured rectilinear mesh of Nm cells, yielding the linear forward system:

d = G · m

where d is the vector of Nd observed SP data (mV), G is the Nd × Nm sensitivity matrix, and m is the Nm-vector of Qₛ values to be recovered.

3.2 Regularised Inversion

The inverse problem is underdetermined (Nm >> Nd) and requires regularisation. SPExplorer implements Tikhonov regularisation with a smoothness penalty:

Φ(m) = ‖Wd(Gm − d)‖² + β ‖Wm · m‖²

where Wd is a data weighting matrix (typically diagonal, with entries proportional to the inverse of estimated data uncertainty), Wm is a model regularisation operator (incorporating depth weighting and spatial smoothness), and β is the regularisation parameter controlled by a cooling schedule. The solution is obtained by iteratively minimising Φ(m) using a conjugate gradient method applied to the normal equations.

3.3 Depth Weighting

Without depth weighting, the inversion places sources preferentially at shallow depth due to the 1/r decay of the Green's function kernel. Following Li & Oldenburg (1998), a depth weighting function w(z) is applied to counteract this tendency:

w(z) = (z + z₀)^(−β/2)    where β ≈ 2 for SP sources

This ensures that model cells at depth receive equitable sensitivity weighting relative to shallow cells, permitting recovery of deep source structures that would otherwise be suppressed.

4. Results & Discussion: Tongariro Volcanic Centre
4.1 Survey Overview and Inversion Setup

The SP dataset from the Tongariro Volcanic Centre (TVC), North Island, New Zealand, was acquired across a 56 km² survey area with more than 4,700 station measurements. The TVC hosts an active hydrothermal system with fumarole fields, acid crater lakes, and documented geothermal fluid upflow aligned with volcanic vent structures. The SP anomaly pattern is strongly controlled by electrokinetic and thermoelectric sources associated with active hydrothermal circulation.

The 3D inversion was performed on a 77 × 68 × 64 cell mesh (335,104 total cells, 77 × 68 × 64 = 335,104 active after topographic trimming). The cell size was 100 m horizontally and variable vertically, with 10 padding cells applied at all boundaries to minimise edge effects. The survey mean elevation is 1,569 m asl; the maximum model elevation is 2,265 m asl (summit) and the minimum is 747 m asl (peripheral survey boundary).

4.2 Inversion Convergence

The inversion converged to a final RMS misfit of 31.4 mV against a peak-to-peak data range of approximately 4,000 mV (−1,500 to +2,500 mV). The SymLogNorm source model reference (ℓ_model) was set to 3.72 × 10⁻⁸ A·m⁻³, providing symmetric positive and negative Qₛ resolution across six orders of magnitude. The residual map is spatially uncorrelated and spectrally white, confirming that the model has been fitted to the signal rather than to noise.

4.3 E–W Section Interpretation

The six E–W vertical sections (Y = 0.79 to 6.19 km N) reveal a strongly heterogeneous Qₛ distribution in the upper 400 m, transitioning to a simpler, broadly positive background at depths of 500–800 m below surface. The northern sections (Y = 0.79–2.79 km) are dominated by sub-vertical negative Qₛ columns to 300–400 m depth, flanked by positive halos — a pattern consistent with focused hydrothermal upflow through permeable conduits driving electrokinetic streaming potentials. The central and southern sections (Y = 4.19–6.19 km) show a westward-deepening positive Qₛ body likely associated with the lateral discharge limb of the hydrothermal convection system.

4.4 N–S Section Interpretation

The N–S sections (X = 0.89 to 6.99 km E) provide orthogonal constraints on the 3D Qₛ geometry. The section at X = 2.09 km contains the most diagnostic feature of the entire dataset: a strongly negative Qₛ anomaly (blue) centred at approximately 500 m depth and 2–4 km northing, reaching amplitudes of −3.7 × 10⁻³ A·m⁻³. This feature is flanked by positive Qₛ above and to the north, forming a coherent electrochemical cell geometry consistent with either a reducing hydrothermal fluid reservoir or a sulphide-bearing mineralisation system at depth. Discrimination between these interpretations would require integration with coincident IP or EM data.

4.5 Current Flow Analysis

Current flow streamlines computed from the recovered Qₛ vector field reveal a three-tier flow architecture consistent with published conceptual models of volcanic hydrothermal systems:

  • Shallow recharge tier (0–200 m): Lateral inward flow from high-elevation flanks toward the volcanic edifice axis, driven by topographic hydraulic head and consistent with meteoric recharge
  • Mid-crustal transport tier (200–500 m): Sub-horizontal streamlines along permeable horizons, likely fault- or stratigraphy-controlled, routing fluid toward upflow conduits
  • Deep return flow tier (500–800 m): Complex multi-directional flow associated with deep hydrothermal convection and possibly magmatic volatile input

This three-tier architecture, recovered non-parametrically from the SP inversion, is consistent with the geochemical zonation observed at TVC fumarole fields and provides the first spatially continuous representation of the hydrothermal plumbing system geometry.

5. Broader Applications
5.1 Sulphide Mineral Exploration

The geobattery model predicts negative SP anomalies (and negative Qₛ in the upper ore body) above oxidising massive sulphide systems. 3D SP inversion provides quantitative depth estimates and source body geometry, substantially improving drill target ranking compared with conventional anomaly peak picking. Applicable deposit types include VMS, SEDEX, magmatic nickel sulphides, and orogenic gold systems with sulphide alteration halos.

5.2 Geothermal Exploration

Thermoelectric and electrokinetic SP signals in geothermal environments allow imaging of upflow conduits, permeable fracture zones, and reservoir boundaries. 3D inversion moves beyond traditional SP low = upflow / SP high = recharge interpretive rules, providing depth-resolved flow geometry applicable to resource assessment and well siting decisions.

5.3 Groundwater and Environmental Applications

Streaming potentials generated by groundwater flow provide continuous information on recharge zones, discharge zones, aquifer connectivity, and preferential flow pathways. Time-lapse 3D SP inversion can monitor dam seepage, landfill leachate migration, CO₂ sequestration fluid migration, and acid mine drainage evolution without borehole intervention.

6. Conclusions

Three-dimensional Self-Potential inversion transforms a qualitative geophysical technique into a quantitative subsurface imaging tool with applications spanning mineral exploration, geothermal resource assessment, hydrogeology, and environmental monitoring. The SPExplorer inversion engine — validated on the Tongariro Volcanic Centre dataset — demonstrates that volumetric source current density models with more than 300,000 cells can be recovered at field-quality RMS misfits, resolving complex hydrothermal flow architectures to depths exceeding 800 m below surface.

The recovered Qₛ models, combined with current flow streamline analysis, provide genuinely new subsurface information not available from any other passive geophysical technique at equivalent survey cost. Integration with complementary methods (ERT, IP, EM, magnetics) further constrains geological interpretation and reduces drilling uncertainty.
